TROMPE-L'ŒIL — PRODUCT-FIRST CREATIVE ENGINE

SELECTED PRODUCT: {PRODUCT}


PRIMARY OBJECTIVE — TROMPE-L'ŒIL

Your primary objective is TROMPE-L'ŒIL:

Create flat printed artwork that convincingly deceives the eye about the physical reality of the selected product or its immediate surroundings.

The viewer should momentarily believe that something physically exists, changes, continues, disappears, opens, protrudes, recedes, transforms, or interacts with reality when it actually exists only as printed artwork.

FIRST FOOL THE EYE.

Then, when possible, fool the mind too through interaction, humor, double meaning, surprise, or discovery.

Do not substitute a merely attractive illustration, clever graphic, decorative pattern, or conceptual image for genuine trompe-l'œil.

The deception itself is the mission.


FRESH-CONCEPT RULE

Every generation must be a completely new invention.

Forget all previous designs.

Do not repair, improve, simplify, continue, reinterpret, or vary a previous concept.

Discard previous subjects, settings, scenery, characters, objects, compositions, jokes, metaphors, and illusion mechanisms.

A rejected concept is DEAD.

Begin again from only:

SELECTED PRODUCT: {PRODUCT}

Approach the product as though a completely new creative team has received it for the first time.


PRODUCT INTERROGATION

Before imagining artwork, examine the real physical product.

Ask:

• What is it physically?
• What are its shape, dimensions, thickness, surfaces, edges, boundaries, orientation, and proportions?
• Where does it normally exist?
• What normally surrounds it?
• How is it actually used?
• What does the user physically do with it?
• What moves during use?
• What stays stationary?
• What real objects or body parts naturally approach, touch, cover, uncover, cross, rotate around, rest upon, enter, leave, or otherwise interact with it?
• What becomes hidden or revealed during normal use?
• From what viewpoint is it normally seen?
• What shadows, reflections, perspective, textures, or environmental relationships naturally occur around it?
• What does the viewer expect this physical object to be?
• What could printed artwork make the viewer mistakenly believe instead?
• What other real object, opening, surface, material, structure, space, or environment could this product convincingly appear to become?
• What words, idioms, double meanings, jokes, rituals, emotions, or cultural associations naturally belong to it?

Do not assume you understand its creative possibilities merely because you recognize the object.

Mentally simulate a real person using the product from beginning to end.


TROMPE-L'ŒIL INTERROGATION

Now ask:

"What could this flat printed surface convincingly pretend to BE?"

"What could appear to exist ABOVE it?"

"What could appear to exist BELOW it?"

"What could appear to exist INSIDE it?"

"What could appear to continue BEYOND it?"

"What could appear to be seen THROUGH it?"

"What could make part of the real product seem missing?"

"What could make its surface appear transparent, open, recessed, raised, broken, dimensional, transformed, disguised, or continuous with its surroundings?"

"What false physical reality could the viewer believe for a moment?"

"What could make the selected product itself appear to become another object?"

Use realistic perspective, scale, lighting, texture, shadows, reflections, occlusion, depth cues, and environmental continuity to make the deception convincing.

Trompe-l'œil is not merely a picture of something dimensional.

The REAL PRODUCT must participate in the deception.

If exactly the same picture would work just as well as a poster or ordinary illustration, reject it.


REALITY IS PART OF THE ARTWORK

The finished artwork will exist on a real physical product in the real world.

Reality may provide part of the illusion.

During reasoning, imagine every real object, body part, movement, shadow, surface, liquid, opening, device, environmental feature, or other physical participant naturally present during use.

Use those elements while THINKING.

Design around their real position, size, movement, orientation, and behavior.

But remember:

THE PRINTED ARTWORK IS NOT NECESSARILY THE ENTIRE FINISHED IMAGE.

Reality can complete it.


PUNCHLINE 101

The artwork creates the setup.

Reality may deliver the punchline.

If a real-world object, body part, action, movement, or environmental feature will naturally complete the deception during normal use, that element is RESERVED FOR REALITY.

DO NOT DEPICT A PRINTED DUPLICATE OF IT.

This is mandatory.

The missing element may be the most important part of the illusion.

During reasoning, imagine the complete interaction.

Then remove from the printable artwork anything that reality will naturally provide.

Do not replace it with an illustrated equivalent.

Do not demonstrate the intended interaction.

Do not explain the illusion.


TRUST THE JOKE

Think like a skilled comedian.

Construct the setup carefully and then stop talking.

The viewer's discovery is part of the experience.

Do not add demonstration props, arrows, motion marks, diagrams, instructional symbols, explanatory labels, explanatory text, or redundant visual clues simply because you are afraid the viewer will not understand.

Before rendering, ask:

"Am I showing anything primarily because I am afraid the viewer won't get it?"

If YES, remove it.

Then ask:

"Without that explanation, will normal use allow the viewer to discover the illusion?"

If YES, trust the concept.

If NO, reject the concept and invent a stronger one.

Never rescue a weak idea by explaining it.

The desired reaction is:

"Wait... OH!"

The artwork should not explain the trick before the viewer experiences it.


PHYSICAL CONTINUITY TEST

Mentally perform normal real-world use before accepting the concept.

Do not evaluate the illusion only in one perfect frozen position.

Move everything that normally moves.

Cover what normally becomes covered.

Reveal what normally becomes revealed.

Lift, place, rotate, slide, touch, carry, or otherwise manipulate whatever normal use requires.

Watch what happens to the illusion.

Stationary printed artwork cannot physically follow a moving real-world element.

If normal movement destroys the physical logic of the illusion, reject the entire concept.

Do not repair it.

Movement should preserve the illusion, strengthen it, meaningfully transform it, reveal something, conceal something, or itself become part of the illusion.


PRODUCT-DEPENDENCE TEST

Ask:

"If this exact artwork were placed on an unrelated product, would the illusion work almost as well?"

If YES, reject it.

Then ask:

"Does the physical identity, geometry, environment, behavior, or normal use of THIS product create or complete the deception?"

The answer should clearly be YES.


FINAL TROMPE-L'ŒIL TEST

Before rendering, identify:

1. WHAT THE PRODUCT REALLY IS.
2. WHAT THE VIEWER IS SUPPOSED TO BELIEVE IT IS OR WHAT IS HAPPENING TO IT.
3. WHAT VISUAL DECEPTION CAUSES THAT FALSE BELIEF.
4. WHAT REALITY CONTRIBUTES.
5. WHAT MUST BE LEFT OUT OF THE PRINTED ARTWORK SO REALITY CAN COMPLETE THE EFFECT.

If there is no convincing false physical perception, reject the concept.

If it is merely attractive artwork, reject it.

If it merely depicts something associated with the product, reject it.

If the product is only functioning as a canvas, reject it.

If the viewer's eye is genuinely deceived by the relationship between the artwork and the real product, continue.


FINAL SELECTION

Internally invent several substantially different concepts.

Reject anything generic.

Reject anything resembling a previous generation.

Reject anything that fails during actual physical use.

Reject anything requiring explanation.

Reject anything where the real product is merely carrying a picture.

Choose the strongest surviving trompe-l'œil concept.

Create ONLY the printable artwork.

Do not create a demonstration mockup.

Do not show how the trick works.

Do not step on the punchline.

FIRST FOOL THE EYE.

THEN LET REALITY COMPLETE THE LIE.

TROMPE-L'ŒIL.
